*Editorial guide · Updated November 15, 2026*

August is the month that requires the most contingency thinking. It usually combines the heaviest heat of the year with the start of typhoon season, and both can disrupt a trip built on fixed bookings. It also has one genuine advantage: many of Seoul's own residents leave in the first part of the month, so the city is often quieter than the weather suggests. This page covers what changes in August and how to build a trip that survives a bad-weather day.

## What the weather actually means for your days

August is usually the hottest month in Seoul and the most humid, and the heat is the kind that stays with you after you go indoors. Daytime outdoor activity needs to be short and shaded, and the evening is usually the only comfortable window for a long walk.

Around the last part of the month, typhoon season usually begins. A typhoon passing near Korea does not have to make landfall to affect your plans: heavy rain bands, strong wind, flooding on low-lying streets and transport suspensions are the usual effects. The [weather-flexible plan](/seoul-rain-and-weather-plan/) is the one to read first if you are in the city in August, because its logic changes when the problem is wind and flooding rather than an ordinary shower.

Useful official warnings come from the national public information portal at [Korea.kr](https://www.korea.kr) and from [VISITKOREA](https://english.visitkorea.or.kr). If a typhoon warning is issued, follow it rather than the itinerary.

## What to do in this month

Treat the first half of the month and the second half as two different trips. Early August is hot but predictable, and it is also when domestic travel peaks, which means the city is emptier and services are under more pressure than at other times of the year. Late August is when the weather risk rises and the city is at its quietest.

With heat and possible storms as the constraints, an August trip works best with indoor anchors that are genuinely worth a morning: the museums, the palace museums, the art spaces, the markets and the long lunches. The [culture-at-your-own-pace guide](/seoul-culture-at-your-pace/) is built for that, and the [design neighbourhood guide](/seoul-design-neighborhood-plan/) works well for a cooler morning of shops and cafes before the heat arrives.

Do not build a day that depends on a single outdoor anchor. If your only plan for a morning is a palace courtyard, a storm takes the day. Add one thing that works under a roof, and keep the second half of the day flexible enough to move.

## What to wear and carry

Very light, loose, quick-drying clothing, and nothing heavy. Pack more of it than you think you need, because laundry takes a day in humid weather and a soaked shirt is not something you want to sit in. A hat, sunscreen and a water bottle are essential rather than optional.

For storms, a compact umbrella is not much help. A light rain shell that covers your bag is better, and a dry bag for phones and documents is worth the space. Shoes with grip matter more in August than in any other month, and carry a thin layer for the strong air conditioning indoors.

## Days to plan around

The first part of August is the busiest domestic travel period in Korea, with heavy traffic on the roads into and out of the city and much fuller services than in late August. If you travel then, leave slack around your departure.

Weekends are hot and busy, and the last weekend of a month usually carries the largest programme of events, which in August may also be moved indoors or cancelled by the organiser. The [weekly listings](/seoul-weekly/) show the dated programme; treat anything outdoors as something to reconfirm with the organiser on the day.

Because typhoon disruption can move your schedule by a whole day, avoid putting an important booked activity on the last day of the trip. If you are arriving, the [arrival plan](/seoul-arrival-plan/) is worth reading before you land, because road and rail conditions around a storm change quickly.
