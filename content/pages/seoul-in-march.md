*Editorial guide · Updated November 15, 2026*

March is where Seoul changes. The cold loosens, the daylight stretches, and the blossom usually arrives in the last third of the month rather than on a date you can plan around. That gap is why March trips sometimes disappoint visitors who expected pink trees on the first day. This page covers how the month behaves and how to avoid the worst blossom crowds.

## What the weather actually means for your days

March is a transition month, and a good day can be genuinely warm while the next is cold enough for a coat. Mornings stay cool, afternoons often lose the heavy winter feeling, and the wind picks up because spring in Seoul comes with air moving between the mountains and the city. Layering is not advice here; it is the practical solution.

Rain falls as short showers more often than as long washes, which makes March more forgiving than the summer months. The air can also carry fine dust that leaves the afternoon hazy until a front passes. The [weather-flexible Seoul plan](/seoul-rain-and-weather-plan/) explains how to keep an indoor anchor close to an outdoor one.

## What to do in this month

Blossom is the headline, and the honest advice is to treat it as a moving target rather than a calendar date. The Korea Tourism Organization publishes current blossom forecasts on [VISITKOREA](https://english.visitkorea.or.kr), and local authorities publish their own timing notices through [Korea.kr](https://www.korea.kr). Check them a week before you travel and again in the week you arrive.

Where you stand matters more than when you arrive. Famous parks and photogenic riverbanks fill at midday and at weekends, while smaller neighbourhood parks and ordinary residential streets often look just as good with nobody in them. Our [design and neighbourhood guide](/seoul-design-neighborhood-plan/) treats a district as a day rather than a landmark list.

Because the blossom usually peaks late, many visitors arrive in the second half of March to find bare branches. If your dates are fixed, build the trip on the parts of the month that do not depend on flowers: the palaces in low morning light and the museums that open new exhibitions in spring.

## What to wear and carry

A light waterproof jacket over layers is the correct answer for most of March. You will want a thinner layer in the middle of the day and something warmer again in the early evening, because the swing between morning and night is wide. Comfortable walking shoes matter more than in winter, because the blossom walking season is long and the ground is often wet.

Carry a compact umbrella and a light scarf — the umbrella for showers, the scarf for the wind. If you are coming from a colder home country, you will still be glad you packed one warm layer for the first and last day.

## Days to plan around

Weekends are the problem in blossom season. The most-photographed spots are at their worst from Saturday morning to Sunday afternoon, and transport around them gets slower. If you can, aim for weekday early mornings and weekday afternoons, when the same places are usually empty.

The last weekend of a month is usually the busiest of the three, because it carries both the blossom crowds and the largest programme of events. Our [weekly listings](/seoul-weekly/) show what is scheduled, so you can see whether the month is unusually full.

March is also the run-up to the university term, so campuses begin to fill with new students and the neighbourhoods around them get busier. A family arriving with teenagers will find plenty to do; our [family plan](/seoul-family-trip-plan/) suggests indoor anchors and a shorter day.
