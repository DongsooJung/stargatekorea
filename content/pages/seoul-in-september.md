*Editorial guide · Updated November 15, 2026*

September is a month of change in Seoul. The worst of the summer heat usually passes, the air starts to clear, and the first autumn colours appear in the high places. It is also the month when a movable lunar holiday usually lands, and that changes how the whole country travels. This page covers what September feels like on the ground and how to handle a holiday that moves each year.

## What the weather actually means for your days

The direction of travel in September is usually downwards: cooler mornings, less oppressive humidity, and clearer air. The first weeks can still carry summer heat and the tail end of typhoon activity, so the month is not uniformly autumnal. Check the forecast for the specific week you are in rather than planning the whole month on a season label.

By the later part of September, the light is different. Haze is thinner, distances are visible again, and the colours in the parks begin to turn in the higher areas first. That combination — cooler, clearer, still busy — is why many repeat visitors rate September above every other month.

Rain still happens, and it can still be heavy, but the long humid stretches of the plum rain and midsummer are usually behind you. The [weather-flexible Seoul plan](/seoul-rain-and-weather-plan/) covers how to move a day without losing it.

## What to do in this month

Autumn is the season for walking, and the Han River paths and the mountains near the city both reward it. The riverfront neighbourhoods are pleasant all day in September, and the hills above the city usually show colour before the city itself does. If you want the leaves, plan slightly later than you would think, and check the regional timing reports on [VISITKOREA](https://english.visitkorea.or.kr) and through [Korea.kr](https://www.korea.kr).

The city has its own September rhythm. Universities start the new term, so the campuses and the neighbourhoods around them fill with students again, and the city feels younger than it did through the summer break. Campus orientation events and club activities usually run for a few weeks and are often open to visitors, subject to each university's own rules.

Food changes with the season. The autumn harvest arrives in the markets, and the Chuseok table is usually the most visible example. Our [food neighbourhood plan](/seoul-food-neighborhood-plan/) is useful for building an evening around a market rather than around a restaurant, which is how you experience a Korean holiday properly if you are here for one.

## What to wear and carry

September asks you to be ready for both seasons. A light jacket with a layer underneath works for most of the month, and it becomes a genuine coat by the end of it. Bring something for the rain, and comfortable shoes for long riverside and hillside walks.

As the humidity drops, you may need less water but more covering, particularly in the early morning and late evening. A thin layer for the evening is worth packing even if the daytime feels warm, and a scarf is more useful than you expect on a clear September night.

## Days to plan around

Chuseok, the harvest festival, usually falls in September or October, and because it follows the lunar calendar its position moves from year to year. It is one of the largest travel periods in Korea: trains and buses fill, and many families travel for several days. Check the official public holiday calendar on [Korea.kr](https://www.korea.kr) and the [Visit Korea holiday pages](https://english.visitkorea.or.kr) to see where it falls, then adjust your bookings.

Outside the holiday, September is calmer than October, and the last weekend of a month usually carries the busiest programme of events as the autumn season gets going. Our [weekly listings](/seoul-weekly/) show what is running.

Around the Chuseok period, which usually fills trains and buses, allow more time than usual and keep your last day free of anything you cannot afford to lose.
